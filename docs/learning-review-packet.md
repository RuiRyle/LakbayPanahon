### prologue-mission3: Archaeological Evidence of Early Human Presence

Objective: Connect a named archaeological site and find with a supported conclusion while recognizing uncertainty and respectful care.

#### prologue-mission3-scene1: Your Mission: Locate the Evidence

You have learned how land and shorelines can change. Now investigate a different question: how do we know people lived in the islands long ago? Your mission is to build a careful museum explanation using a place, a find and what that find supports.

Archaeology studies past life through objects, remains and the places where they are found. Context means the recorded setting: location, layer and relationship to other finds. A find separated from its context can lose useful information.

Start with the locator. Tabon is at Lipuun Point in Quezon, Palawan. Callao Cave is in Peñablanca, Cagayan, in northern Luzon. These dots locate two sites, not every place with evidence of early people. They do not show an ancient travel route between the caves.

#### prologue-mission3-scene2: Watch How: Make a Tabon Evidence Card

Tabon Cave in Palawan has yielded human remains, including evidence of Homo sapiens, and many stone tools. Homo sapiens is the scientific name of our species. A stone tool is an object shaped or used by people; it is not itself a human bone.

Worked example: turn a source description into an evidence card. Step 1. Name the place: Tabon, Palawan. Step 2. Name the find: human remains identified as Homo sapiens. Step 3. State a supported conclusion: people of our species were present there long ago.

Now add a limit. This find does not identify every ancestor of every Filipino. Different finds can come from different times, so do not give every Tabon object the same age. The illustration is a summary card, not a photograph of an original find.

#### prologue-mission3-scene3: Try Together: Compare Callao and Tabon

Callao Cave in Cagayan has yielded ancient teeth and bones studied by a research team. The researchers described Homo luzonensis as a distinct ancient human species in 2019. It is not simply another name for Homo sapiens. A fossil is a preserved remain or trace of past life. A species is a scientific grouping studied through features and relationships. Scientists compare features and other evidence when making such identifications.

Compare the cards together. Both refer to human evidence from caves. Their locations and species identifications differ. Tabon is on Palawan; Callao is on Luzon.

A 2019 University of the Philippines report described the Callao fossils as about 50,000 to 67,000 years old. That is an age report about studied material, not the year scientists found it and not proof of the first arrival of people anywhere in the islands. New work may refine explanations. No appearance or full life story is reconstructed in these teaching cards.

#### prologue-mission3-scene4: Your Turn: Read Time Carefully

Time information answers different questions. A research timeline tells us when modern study happened: National Museum investigations at Tabon included 1962 to 1965; the Homo luzonensis description was published in 2019. These dates do not mean the ancient people lived then.

An age estimate describes how old a find may be. A range gives limits rather than one exact year. With years ago, a larger number means farther into the past.

Try the clearly fictional range exercise. Find A is estimated at 1,000 to 2,000 years ago. Find B is estimated at 1,500 to 2,500 years ago. The ranges overlap from 1,500 to 2,000.

You cannot be certain which find is older using these ranges alone. These are made-up numbers for practice, not dates for Tabon or Callao. The oldest evidence found so far is not necessarily the first person who arrived.

#### prologue-mission3-scene5: Make a Choice: Protect the Record

A visitor notices a possible old object at a managed site. It is tempting to pick it up, clean it or take it as a souvenir. Those actions can damage the object or remove information about its setting. Leave it in place and tell the responsible site staff. Students should not excavate or test finds themselves.

Human remains are preserved parts of people who lived in the past. They are not game prizes, props or objects to handle for entertainment. Care involves responsible specialists and respect for communities connected with heritage.

You may save an authored explanation in your Evidence Journal. That bookmark is a learning reference, not ownership of an original object. A photo, copy or teaching card should be labelled honestly. Pictures of models do not become original archaeological evidence.

#### prologue-mission3-scene6: Field Journal: Build Your Museum Explanation

Your museum explanation should connect evidence with a claim, not just collect impressive names. Use this structure: at this place, researchers studied this find; it supports this conclusion; it does not tell us everything.

Connect the mission ideas. The locator distinguishes Tabon in Palawan from Callao in Cagayan. Evidence cards distinguish find types and species identifications. Research dates differ from ancient ages. Estimated ranges can leave uncertainty. Context and respectful care preserve opportunities to learn.

Try one final comparison. A report says Homo sapiens remains were studied at Tabon and teeth and bones identified as Homo luzonensis were studied at Callao. You can explain the different records without claiming an exact travel route, one ancestor for everyone, or a complete life story. Save useful opened clues in your journal if you want to revisit them.

### chapter4-mission1: Ancient Writing and Communication

Objective: How can we read writing sources without mixing up scripts, languages and objects?

- https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/2022/10/21/baybayin-ancient-and-traditional-scripts-of-the-philippines-gallery/

#### chapter4-mission1-scene1: Different ways to share a message

Communication means sharing a message or idea. People can communicate through spoken words, stories, signs and writing. These forms can serve different purposes.

An oral account is shared through speech. An inscription is writing marked on an object or surface. Ask who produced each source, in what setting, and what it can help explain.

A missing written record does not mean a community had no knowledge or history. Compare the sources available instead of ranking people by the records that survive.

#### chapter4-mission1-scene2: Read the labelled ka example

A script is a system of written signs. A language includes words and grammar. They are related but different: a language can be written using different scripts.

The teaching card labels one Baybayin sign ka. In this basic example it includes a consonant and an a vowel. It is not simply the English letter K.

Read the label, identify ka, and keep the claim small. The modern typeset sign is not an ancient artifact or a translation of an entire inscription. More signs and marks need further learning.

#### chapter4-mission1-scene3: Compare the copperplate and the teaching card

An inscription is writing marked on an object or surface. The Laguna Copperplate Inscription has writing associated with Kawi and a date read as 900 CE.

Compare it with the modern ka card. One is an inscribed object studied by specialists; the other is a teaching example of a Baybayin sign. Their scripts and source histories differ.

An inscription can help investigate writing in its setting. It does not prove that every person could read or that every community used the same script. A replica should be labelled as a replica.

#### chapter4-mission1-scene4: Writing knowledge continues

A living tradition is a practice people continue to use and pass on. The National Museum highlights Buhid-Mangyan and Hanunuo-Mangyan writing in Mindoro, and Pala'wan and Tagbanua writing in Palawan.

Tradition names and community names should be identified carefully. Do not exchange labels or assume every person in a place has one identical practice.

To share an example, check its source, meaning and any sharing conditions. Living people are not displays frozen in the past. Learning and change can occur together.

#### chapter4-mission1-scene5: Build a reliable source label

An online picture may show an original object, a replica or a new design. A caption should tell you which and identify a source.

If a picture claims to translate an ancient message, ask who identified the script and what supports the reading. Do not invent missing words because the signs look familiar.

Build a careful label: name the source, distinguish original from copy, give a supported description, and state uncertainty where it matters. This connects writing knowledge with evidence.

### prologue-mission2: Formation of the Philippine Islands: Scientific Explanations

Objective: Use a process model and evidence to distinguish land-building from exposure of existing land.

#### prologue-mission2-scene1: Earth Is Changing: Follow the Arrows

Your mission is to explain two different kinds of change: building or moving land, and changing how much land water covers. They can work over very long periods. One drawing cannot explain the whole history of every Philippine island.

A tectonic plate is a large, solid piece of Earth's outer layer. Plates move slowly compared with everyday journeys. They can move toward one another, apart, or past one another. Their interactions can shape mountains, trenches and volcanic regions.

Read the arrows in the three-panel model. They show kinds of movement, not actual Philippine plate boundaries. Rocks and measured movement help scientists investigate these processes. A classroom diagram shows an idea; it is not itself a measurement of an island's age.

#### prologue-mission2-scene2: Watch How: Volcanic Material Can Build Land

Watch a worked explanation using three stages. Magma is molten rock below the surface. When it reaches the surface, it is called lava. Volcanic material can cool and become rock. Material can build up over repeated events.

Step 1. Find the material coming through an opening. Step 2. Compare the smaller pile with the larger pile. Step 3. Explain the change: repeated additions of material can build land.

The stages simplify a process. They do not say how long every volcano takes to grow, and an eruption can also damage or remove land. Volcanism is part of the Philippines' complex geological history; not every island is one volcano. This diagram is for learning, not a prediction about a real eruption.

#### chap2-mission1-scene2: Compare Social Position, Duties and Freedom

People could do similar work but have different rights and choices. Social position means a recognized place in society. It could affect what someone was expected to do and what they were free to choose.

One example comes from Juan de Plasencia, a Spanish missionary writing about Tagalog communities in 1589. His account was written after Spanish arrival. It does not describe every Philippine community.

In this account, maharlika were free people with duties to a datu. Aliping namamahay could have their own homes and property while owing service. Aliping sa gigilid served in another household under greater restrictions; the account says they could be sold. These were unequal conditions, not simply different jobs.

Other sources use names such as timawa. Do not assume that groups with different names had the same rights. Compare the place, time, duties and choices. A person's historical position does not measure their worth.

#### chap2-mission1-scene3: Family Ties and Knowledge Keepers

#### chapter15-mission2-scene5: Build the Contribution Exhibit

You now have three sourced cases. Del Pilar translated a text so it could be available in Tagalog. De los Reyes founded El Ilocano. The Malolos women requested permission for a school. The cases show different ways people used skills, organization and support to take part in public life.

Build the exhibit by matching each action with its contribution. Match the evidence, not the person's fame or wealth. The translation concerns language access. The newspaper concerns preparing a publication. The request concerns an attempt to expand schooling.

These actions did not all happen in one year or guarantee the same result. A request is an action toward a goal; a publication makes material available but does not prove every reader's response. After the matches fit, explain why more than one kind of contribution belongs in the exhibit.

#### chapter15-mission2-scene6: Explain the Exhibit to Another Learner

Prepare a short explanation for another Grade 5 learner. Start with an action, then connect it with a public concern. Try: Del Pilar contributed by ___. De los Reyes contributed by ___. The Malolos women contributed by ___. Resources and education could help people ___.

Keep the selected dates in order: the translation appeared in 1882, the Malolos request was made in 1888, and El Ilocano was founded in 1889. Explain the connection instead of only reciting years. Writers, translators and organized learners made different contributions. Their examples do not mean everyone had equal resources or that people without travel opportunities lacked knowledge.
